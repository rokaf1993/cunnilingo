East End rhyming slang works by swapping a word for a phrase that rhymes with it, then dropping the rhyming word so only the innocent half is said out loud. That makes it well suited to this subject. These are the entries that touch on it.

6 entries · the act, the anatomy and the equipment

The act

plate of ham
rhymes with gam → oral sex
says plateSlang

Gam is a worn-down gamahucher, the French verb listed under French in the dictionary, so the chain runs French to Victorian pornography to the East End. It became a verb of its own: plating. Sources split on whether a plate of ham means cunnilingus or fellatio, and most read it as oral sex either way.

The anatomy

grumble and grunt
rhymes with cunt → the vulva
says grumbleVulgar

First recorded in the 1930s. The short form drifted: on its own, grumble came to mean pornography. Groan, growl and gasp all stand in for grumble.

Berkeley Hunt
rhymes with cunt → the vulva
says berkVulgar

A real foxhunt in Gloucestershire. This one escaped into ordinary English: berk is now a mild word for an idiot, mild precisely because almost nobody using it knows what it is short for. Also Berkshire Hunt and Burlington Hunt.

Jack and Danny
rhymes with fanny → the vulva
says jackSlang

Also Jacky Danny. The later and tamer sense of fanny, meaning nonsense or stalling, rides along with it, so a straight answer with no Jack and Danny means one with no waffle.

The equipment

north and south
rhymes with mouth → the mouth
says northEveryday

Carried by the 1906 music hall song What a Mouth (What a North and South), sung by Harry Champion. Hotten's 1859 slang dictionary has the older east and south.

Hampstead Heath
rhymes with teeth → the teeth
says HampsteadsEveryday

Named for the heath north of London. Victorian slang used Hounslows instead, after the heath on the other side of the city.

The same rhyme, other dressings. One rhyme word carries most of this vocabulary, and the phrases built on it are close to endless: Charlie Hunt (said as Charlie), Joe Hunt (Joey), Sir Anthony Blunt, sharp and blunt, eyes front, National Front and all quiet, from All Quiet on the Western Front. These come from a single survey of the word's history rather than from several independent records, so treat them as less firmly attested than the entries above.

Sources: Brewer's Dictionary of London Phrase & Fable via Oxford Reference, Green's Dictionary of Slang, Julian Franklyn's A Dictionary of Rhyming Slang, and Wikipedia on the Berkeley Hunt. Rhyming slang is spoken rather than written, so datings are approximate and usage varies.
